## Foreword

Artificial intelligence is becoming embedded in business processes, software products, security operations, infrastructure, decision support, and customer experiences. Its benefits are accompanied by risks that do not fit neatly within a single technology or assurance discipline. AI governance therefore requires coordinated business ownership, enterprise architecture, security, privacy, legal, data, risk, operations, and independent assurance.

The Enterprise Secure AI Framework (ESAF) provides a vendor-neutral management and operating standard for that coordination. It does not replace established cybersecurity, privacy, quality, safety, sector, or regulatory obligations. It establishes the enterprise system through which those obligations can be applied consistently to AI capabilities.

ESAF is organized around three mutually reinforcing pillars:

- **Protect AI** establishes security, privacy, safety, resilience, and trustworthy-operation requirements.
- **Utilize AI** establishes requirements for responsible adoption, delivery, workforce enablement, operations, and value realization.
- **Govern AI** establishes accountability, portfolio oversight, risk management, compliance, transparency, and assurance.

The core standard defines the management system and its required outcomes. Detailed controls, assessment procedures, reference architectures, implementation guidance, data models, crosswalks, and profiles are maintained in companion publications.

## How to use this standard

Organizations should use ESAF-1000 to establish or evaluate their enterprise AI management system. A typical adoption sequence is:

1. Define organizational context, scope, and interested parties.
2. Establish accountability, policy, governance bodies, and decision rights.
3. Inventory and classify AI capabilities.
4. Select risk-proportionate requirements, controls, profiles, and lifecycle gates.
5. Implement and operate the selected controls.
6. Retain evidence and assess effectiveness.
7. Report outcomes and continuously improve the management system.

ESAF-1000 uses outcome-oriented requirements. ESAF-1100 supplies detailed controls and assessment procedures. An organization may integrate ESAF into existing management systems rather than create a separate governance structure where existing structures can meet the requirements.

## 1. Scope

### 1.1 Purpose

ESAF-1000 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an enterprise system for governing, securing, adopting, operating, and assuring AI.

### 1.2 Applicability

This standard applies to AI capabilities that an organization develops, acquires, configures, integrates, provides, operates, or materially uses. It includes, without limitation:

- machine learning and predictive systems;
- generative and foundation-model applications;
- large and small language models;
- retrieval-augmented generation and enterprise search;
- copilots, assistants, and decision-support systems;
- autonomous and agentic systems;
- embedded AI in commercial products and services;
- AI-assisted software development and security operations;
- analytical, optimization, vision, speech, and recommendation systems;
- internally hosted, cloud, hybrid, edge, and third-party AI services.

### 1.3 Boundaries

The organization shall document the organizational, legal, geographic, technical, data, lifecycle, and supply-chain boundaries of its ESAF scope. Exclusions shall be justified, approved, periodically reviewed, and shall not be used to avoid an applicable legal, contractual, safety, privacy, or security obligation.

### 1.4 Relationship to other obligations

ESAF supplements rather than supersedes applicable law, regulation, contract, policy, or sector-specific requirements. When requirements conflict, the organization shall identify the conflict, obtain qualified legal or compliance guidance, document the resolution, and apply the more protective requirement where lawful and appropriate.

### 2.2 Risk-proportionate application

Where this standard requires measures to be proportionate to risk, the organization shall use documented criteria that consider at least impact, likelihood, uncertainty, autonomy, data sensitivity, affected persons, external exposure, operational criticality, reversibility, and applicable obligations.

### 2.3 Evidence

Evidence shall be attributable, sufficiently complete, protected from unauthorized modification, retained for a defined period, and available to authorized reviewers. Evidence may be automated or manual and may be inherited from shared enterprise services when inheritance is documented and validated.

## 3. Terms and definitions

The repository glossary is authoritative for project terminology. The following terms are central to ESAF-1000.

### 3.1 AI capability

A business or operational function that uses AI to produce or materially influence an outcome.

### 3.2 AI system

The models, data, software, infrastructure, identities, integrations, workflows, people, processes, and governance used to deliver an AI capability.

### 3.3 AI asset

An identifiable component supporting an AI system, including a model, dataset, prompt, embedding, vector store, agent, tool, API, knowledge source, or runtime environment.

### 3.4 Accountable owner

The individual with authority and responsibility for an AI capability's approved purpose, outcomes, risk, resources, and lifecycle decisions.

### 3.5 Human oversight

Meaningful human authority to understand, supervise, challenge, approve, intervene in, or stop AI behavior according to risk.

### 3.6 Material change

A change that could alter the intended purpose, risk classification, affected population, data use, model behavior, autonomy, architecture, supplier, legal obligations, or control effectiveness of an AI capability.

### 3.7 Residual risk

Risk remaining after selected treatments and controls are applied.

### 3.8 Guiding principles

The enterprise AI management system shall be designed and operated according to these principles:

- **Human accountability:** people and organizational authorities remain accountable for AI-enabled outcomes.
- **Secure and private by design:** security and privacy are integrated throughout the lifecycle.
- **Risk-proportionate governance:** oversight, controls, evidence, and assurance scale with risk.
- **Purpose limitation:** AI is used only for approved, documented, and lawful purposes.
- **Transparency and traceability:** capabilities, ownership, material decisions, limitations, and evidence are discoverable and reviewable.
- **Defense in depth:** no single administrative or technical safeguard is treated as sufficient.
- **Continuous assurance:** performance, behavior, value, risk, and compliance are monitored after deployment.
- **Responsible innovation:** governance enables experimentation and adoption within explicit boundaries.

## 4. Organizational context and management-system scope

### 4.1 Organizational context

The organization shall identify internal and external factors relevant to its AI objectives and risks. Analysis shall consider, as applicable:

- business strategy, culture, risk appetite, and operating model;
- enterprise architecture, cybersecurity, privacy, data, and sourcing strategies;
- workforce capacity and competence;
- customers, employees, partners, communities, and other affected persons;
- applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, ethical, and sector obligations;
- threat, technology, supplier, geopolitical, and market developments;
- environmental, societal, accessibility, and human-rights impacts.

The analysis shall be reviewed at planned intervals and when material changes occur.

### 4.2 Interested parties

The organization shall identify relevant interested parties, their requirements, and how those requirements are addressed. Interested parties may include boards, executives, employees, customers, affected persons, regulators, auditors, suppliers, partners, shareholders, and the public.

### 4.3 Scope statement

The organization shall maintain an approved ESAF scope statement identifying:

- included organizational units, capabilities, services, and locations;
- included lifecycle activities and supply-chain relationships;
- interfaces with other management systems;
- exclusions and their justification;
- the authority that approves and reviews the scope.

### 4.4 Management-system processes

The organization shall define the processes and interactions needed to satisfy ESAF, including criteria, owners, inputs, outputs, evidence, dependencies, metrics, and escalation paths.

## 5. Leadership, policy, and accountability

### 5.1 Leadership commitment

Executive leadership shall demonstrate accountability for the enterprise AI management system by:

- approving its scope, policy, objectives, and risk appetite;
- assigning authorities and accountable owners;
- integrating AI governance with business and enterprise governance;
- providing appropriate resources and competencies;
- reviewing performance, material risks, and significant incidents;
- promoting responsible innovation and a culture in which concerns can be raised without retaliation.

### 5.2 Enterprise AI policy

The organization shall maintain an enterprise AI policy that:

- states approved principles and objectives;
- defines scope and applicability;
- establishes acceptable and prohibited uses;
- assigns governance and lifecycle responsibilities;
- requires risk-proportionate security, privacy, safety, and human oversight;
- establishes exception, escalation, incident, and enforcement expectations;
- is communicated, available, version-controlled, and periodically reviewed.

### 5.3 Governance structure

The organization shall establish governance bodies or integrate AI responsibilities into existing bodies. The structure shall provide:

- strategic oversight and investment prioritization;
- cross-functional lifecycle and risk decisions;
- architecture, security, privacy, legal, data, compliance, and operational review;
- independent challenge and assurance appropriate to risk;
- documented decisions, conditions, exceptions, and appeals;
- escalation to executive leadership and the board when material.

### 5.4 Roles and decision rights

The organization shall assign and communicate decision rights for strategy, use-case approval, architecture, data, model selection, risk acceptance, production authorization, incidents, material changes, and retirement. Incompatible responsibilities shall be separated or subject to compensating oversight.

### 5.5 Accountability for each capability

Every in-scope AI capability shall have, at minimum:

- an accountable business owner;
- an accountable technical or service owner;
- identified security, privacy, data, legal, compliance, and operational stakeholders as applicable;
- a defined purpose, users, affected parties, and expected outcomes;
- a lifecycle state, risk classification, review date, and retirement authority.

## 6. Planning and risk management

### 6.1 AI objectives

The organization shall establish measurable AI objectives aligned with business strategy, policy, risk appetite, and stakeholder requirements. Objectives shall identify owners, measures, time frames, required resources, dependencies, and evaluation methods.

### 6.2 AI risk-management method

The organization shall maintain a documented AI risk-management method that:

- applies throughout the lifecycle;
- considers benefits, harms, threats, vulnerabilities, uncertainty, and systemic dependencies;
- addresses organizational, individual, societal, and environmental impacts where relevant;
- assesses inherent and residual risk;
- defines treatment, acceptance, escalation, monitoring, and reassessment criteria;
- permits aggregation and reporting across the portfolio.

### 6.3 Risk dimensions

Risk assessments shall consider dimensions applicable to the capability, including:

- strategic and business risk;
- cybersecurity and adversarial misuse;
- privacy and data protection;
- safety, reliability, resilience, and availability;
- legal, regulatory, contractual, and intellectual-property risk;
- fairness, harmful bias, accessibility, and human-rights impact;
- model, data, software, and supplier provenance;
- operational, financial, reputational, and concentration risk;
- autonomy, agency, tool use, and unintended actions;
- environmental and resource impacts.

### 6.4 Risk classification

Every in-scope capability shall receive a documented risk classification before production use. The classification shall determine required lifecycle gates, controls, evidence, approval authority, monitoring, review frequency, independent assurance, and human oversight.

### 6.5 Opportunity management

The organization should evaluate opportunities alongside risks, including improved service, productivity, resilience, accessibility, safety, decision quality, and public benefit. Benefit claims shall have defined measures and shall not override mandatory risk treatment.

### 6.6 Change planning

Changes to the management system or an AI capability shall be planned and controlled. Material changes shall trigger reassessment, revalidation, and reauthorization proportionate to their effects.

## 7. Support and enabling capabilities

### 7.1 Resources

The organization shall provide resources necessary to operate the management system, including people, funding, platforms, data, testing, monitoring, incident response, and independent assurance.

### 7.2 Competence

Personnel performing work that can affect AI outcomes or risk shall be competent on the basis of appropriate education, training, skills, and experience. Competency requirements and evidence shall be role-based and periodically reviewed.

### 7.3 Awareness and acceptable use

Workers and relevant third parties shall understand the AI policy, permitted tools and data, reporting obligations, limitations of AI output, human-accountability expectations, and consequences of nonconformance.

### 7.4 Communication

The organization shall determine what AI-related information is communicated, to whom, when, by whom, and through which approved channels. Communication requirements shall address internal governance, affected users, incident notification, regulatory reporting, supplier coordination, and public transparency where applicable.

### 7.5 Documented information

Documented information required by ESAF shall be identified, approved, version-controlled, protected, retained, accessible, and disposed of according to defined requirements. The organization shall control records obtained from suppliers and inherited enterprise services.

### 7.6 Shared services and inherited controls

Capabilities may inherit controls from enterprise platforms or suppliers. Inheritance shall identify the provider, scope, responsibility boundary, evidence, dependencies, limitations, and validation method. The consuming capability remains accountable for gaps and correct configuration.

## 8. Enterprise AI portfolio and inventory

### 8.1 Portfolio management

The organization shall manage AI capabilities as an enterprise portfolio. Portfolio decisions shall consider strategy, value, risk, duplication, interoperability, concentration, technical debt, resource demand, and lifecycle status.

### 8.2 AI inventory

The organization shall maintain an authoritative, current inventory of in-scope AI capabilities. Shadow, embedded, experimental, and third-party AI shall be included when within scope.

Each inventory record shall identify, at minimum:

- a unique identifier, name, description, and approved purpose;
- accountable business and technical owners;
- organizational unit, users, affected parties, and deployment status;
- capability class, criticality, risk classification, and lifecycle state;
- provider, model, material components, hosting, and architecture pattern;
- data categories, classifications, sources, and jurisdictions;
- external integrations, agents, tools, and material dependencies;
- approval, review, exception, and retirement dates;
- applicable obligations, profiles, and assurance status.

### 8.3 Model registry and AIBOM

Production AI systems shall maintain model records and an AI Bill of Materials proportionate to risk. Records shall support provenance, change management, vulnerability and incident analysis, impact assessment, reproducibility where required, and retirement.

### 8.4 Dependency mapping

The organization shall identify material upstream and downstream dependencies, including identity, data, models, platforms, suppliers, APIs, tools, infrastructure, monitoring, and business processes. Critical dependencies shall be considered in continuity and concentration-risk planning.

### 8.5 Discovery and reconciliation

The organization shall use risk-proportionate processes to discover unregistered AI use and reconcile inventory records with procurement, architecture, identity, cloud, endpoint, network, data, and financial sources.

## 9. Enterprise AI lifecycle

### 9.1 Lifecycle requirement

Every in-scope capability shall follow the enterprise AI lifecycle. Activities may be iterative or combined, but required decisions and evidence shall not be omitted without an approved exception.

### 9.2 Lifecycle stages

| Stage | Required outcome | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | AI direction aligns with organizational objectives and risk appetite. | Strategy, objectives, portfolio criteria |
| Ideation | A use case, affected parties, preliminary feasibility, and preliminary risk are understood. | Use-case proposal |
| Business Case | Expected value, costs, alternatives, dependencies, and success measures justify investment. | Business case |
| Risk Classification | Inherent risk and required oversight are determined. | Initial risk assessment and classification |
| Architecture | An approved pattern, trust boundaries, integrations, and responsibilities are defined. | Architecture decision record and diagrams |
| Data Readiness | Data is lawful, authorized, suitable, governed, and of sufficient quality. | Data readiness and impact assessments |
| Model Selection | Models are evaluated for purpose, performance, risk, provenance, licensing, and supplier concerns. | Model evaluation and selection record |
| Development | The capability is built or configured under controlled engineering practices. | Technical documentation and change records |
| Validation | Requirements, performance, security, privacy, safety, fairness, resilience, and limitations are evaluated. | Validation report and unresolved findings |
| Approval | Authorized decision-makers accept residual risk and production conditions. | Production authorization |
| Deployment | Approved configuration, monitoring, support, recovery, and user readiness are established. | Deployment record and operational readiness evidence |
| Operations | The service is managed within approved boundaries and objectives. | Runbooks, service records, and operational metrics |
| Monitoring | Behavior, performance, risk, security, compliance, value, and change triggers are observed. | Dashboards, logs, alerts, and review records |
| Continuous Improvement | Findings, incidents, feedback, and changes produce controlled improvements. | Improvement backlog and change evidence |
| Retirement | Access, data, models, dependencies, contracts, and records are safely disposed of or preserved. | Retirement and data-disposition record |

### 9.3 Lifecycle gates

The organization shall establish risk-based gates that authorize progression. At minimum, gates shall cover concept or investment, architecture and data, security and risk, production readiness, periodic recertification, material change, and retirement.

Gate decisions shall identify:

- the capability and version reviewed;
- required reviewers and decision authority;
- evidence evaluated;
- approval, rejection, conditions, or remediation;
- accepted residual risk and expiration;
- the next review or triggering event.

### 9.4 Experimentation

Experiments and proofs of concept may use streamlined governance when segregated from production, restricted to approved data, time-bounded, inventoried, assigned an owner, and prevented from producing unreviewed material outcomes. Promotion beyond the approved experimental boundary shall follow the full applicable lifecycle.

## 10. Protect AI

### 10.1 Objective

The organization shall protect the confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, safety, resilience, and trustworthy behavior of AI systems and the information they process.

### 10.2 Security architecture

AI systems shall use approved security architecture that defines trust boundaries, identities, data flows, models, integrations, external services, administrative paths, logging, and failure modes. Security shall be integrated throughout the lifecycle and use defense in depth.

### 10.3 Identity and access

The organization shall:

- uniquely identify and authenticate human and non-human actors;
- apply least privilege, separation of duties, and periodic access review;
- manage agent, service, workload, API, and administrative identities;
- protect credentials and secrets using approved services;
- constrain delegated authority and privileged actions;
- revoke access promptly when no longer authorized.

### 10.4 Data protection and privacy

The organization shall govern data used or produced by AI according to classification, authorization, purpose, provenance, quality, retention, residency, privacy, and contractual requirements. Protections shall extend to prompts, responses, training and evaluation data, embeddings, vector stores, logs, feedback, and derived information.

### 10.5 Model and supply-chain security

The organization shall establish provenance, integrity, version, approval, storage, dependency, licensing, and vulnerability requirements for models and material components. Untrusted or externally sourced artifacts shall be evaluated before use. Production replacement and rollback shall be controlled.

### 10.6 Application and integration security

AI applications shall apply secure design and engineering practices, including risk-appropriate input handling, instruction and context separation, output validation, authorization of tool calls, session isolation, rate and resource controls, dependency management, and testing for misuse and adversarial behavior.

### 10.7 Infrastructure and platform security

AI workloads shall operate in approved environments with hardened configuration, vulnerability management, network controls, encryption, secrets management, change control, backup, recovery, and capacity safeguards proportionate to risk.

### 10.8 Agentic and autonomous systems

Systems capable of initiating actions shall have:

- a unique accountable owner and managed identity;
- documented purpose, authority, tools, data, memory, and prohibited actions;
- enforceable permission and resource boundaries;
- risk-appropriate human oversight and approval points;
- complete action and decision-path logging where feasible;
- tested intervention, suspension, and recovery mechanisms;
- safeguards against recursive delegation, unauthorized persistence, and uncontrolled propagation.

### 10.9 Monitoring and detection

The organization shall monitor AI systems for risk-relevant events, including unauthorized access or change, adversarial inputs, data exposure, policy violations, drift, degradation, anomalous tool use, unsafe behavior, supplier changes, and resource abuse. Alerts shall have owners, thresholds, response procedures, and testing.

### 10.10 AI incident management

AI incidents shall integrate with enterprise incident management and address AI-specific containment, evidence, model and data dependencies, affected parties, supplier coordination, reporting, recovery, and lessons learned. High-risk systems shall be included in exercises at planned intervals.

### 10.11 Resilience and fail-safe behavior

The organization shall define availability, recovery, degradation, fallback, reversibility, and safe-state requirements. Where AI is unavailable, unreliable, or outside approved conditions, the system shall fail safely or transfer to an approved alternative appropriate to the consequence of failure.

## 11. Utilize AI

### 11.1 Objective

The organization shall enable AI use that is responsible, secure, operationally sustainable, and demonstrably aligned with approved business outcomes.

### 11.2 Use-case selection

AI shall be used for a defined purpose with identified users, beneficiaries, affected parties, limitations, alternatives, and success measures. Technology novelty alone shall not constitute sufficient justification.

### 11.3 Approved services and usage boundaries

The organization shall define approved AI services, configurations, data-use boundaries, prohibited uses, and processes for requesting new capabilities. Restrictions shall be technically enforced where proportionate and supported by education, monitoring, and escalation.

### 11.4 Responsible human use

Users shall understand that accountability remains with authorized people and organizational decision-makers. AI output used for material decisions shall receive review appropriate to its accuracy, uncertainty, impact, and reversibility. The organization shall define when AI output may be used directly, requires verification, or is prohibited.

### 11.5 Engineering and delivery

Teams shall integrate AI into controlled software, data, model, prompt, and agent engineering practices. AI-generated artifacts shall receive review and validation equivalent to their risk and intended use. Confidential code, data, credentials, or intellectual property shall not be submitted to unauthorized services.

### 11.6 Workforce enablement

The organization shall maintain role-based AI literacy and competency for executives, managers, users, developers, data professionals, operators, security personnel, risk functions, and assessors. Training shall address safe use, limitations, data handling, verification, escalation, and relevant obligations.

### 11.7 Operational readiness

Before production authorization, an AI capability shall have defined service ownership, support, monitoring, incident handling, change management, continuity, capacity, user communication, fallback, and retirement arrangements.

### 11.8 Value measurement

The organization shall measure whether AI capabilities achieve approved outcomes. Measures should consider quality, productivity, cost, service, adoption, accessibility, security, risk, user experience, and unintended effects. Capabilities that do not provide sufficient value or remain outside risk tolerance shall be changed, restricted, suspended, or retired.

### 11.9 Responsible scaling

Expansion to new users, data, jurisdictions, decisions, integrations, or autonomy shall be treated as a change and assessed before deployment. Pilot success shall not be assumed to demonstrate readiness for enterprise scale.

## 12. Govern AI

### 12.1 Objective

The organization shall ensure AI remains accountable, transparent, lawful, risk-managed, auditable, and aligned with organizational objectives throughout its lifecycle.

### 12.2 Portfolio oversight

Leadership shall receive sufficient portfolio information to oversee investment, value, material risk, incidents, exceptions, technical and supplier concentration, lifecycle status, and conformance.

### 12.3 Transparency and notices

The organization shall determine and provide transparency appropriate to the context, including when people interact with AI, how AI materially influences outcomes, important limitations, data use, human-review options, and channels to raise concerns. Disclosures shall be accurate, accessible, and consistent with security and legal requirements.

### 12.4 Explainability and traceability

The level of explanation and traceability shall be proportionate to impact, affected parties, decision context, and applicable obligations. The organization shall document when meaningful explanation is not technically feasible and apply alternative safeguards.

### 12.5 Fairness, accessibility, and affected-party impact

The organization shall identify and evaluate reasonably foreseeable differential impacts, harmful bias, exclusion, and accessibility barriers. Evaluation shall use appropriate expertise, representative evidence where lawful and feasible, and documented acceptance or remediation decisions.

### 12.6 Human oversight, challenge, and appeal

The organization shall establish human oversight appropriate to risk. Where AI materially affects people or protected interests, the organization shall provide mechanisms for authorized review, challenge, correction, and appeal as required by law, policy, or risk treatment.

### 12.7 Third-party AI governance

Third-party AI shall be subject to risk-proportionate due diligence, contracting, onboarding, monitoring, change notification, incident notification, data-use restrictions, assurance, continuity, exit, and concentration-risk management. Shared-responsibility boundaries shall be documented.

### 12.8 Legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations

The organization shall maintain a process to identify, interpret, assign, implement, and monitor applicable obligations. Qualified legal and compliance functions shall review material interpretations. Jurisdictional and industry profiles shall be versioned.

### 12.9 Standards crosswalks

External mappings shall identify the exact source version, relationship type, rationale, mapping direction, reviewer, and date. A crosswalk shall not be represented as certification, equivalence, or proof of compliance without the required independent assessment and authority.

### 12.10 Exceptions and risk acceptance

Exceptions shall identify the requirement, scope, justification, risk, compensating measures, owner, approver, expiration, monitoring, and remediation plan. Exceptions shall be time-bounded and reviewed before expiration. Acceptance authority shall be commensurate with residual risk.

### 12.11 Records and accountability

Material governance decisions shall be recorded and traceable to evidence, reviewers, conditions, dissent or unresolved findings where relevant, and accountable decision-makers.

## 13. Performance evaluation and assurance

### 13.1 Measurement framework

The organization shall define what will be monitored and measured, the methods and thresholds used, responsibility, frequency, retention, and reporting. Measures shall cover management-system effectiveness, portfolio value and risk, control performance, capability behavior, incidents, findings, exceptions, and stakeholder outcomes.

### 13.2 Capability review

Each production capability shall undergo review at a frequency determined by risk and upon material triggers. Reviews shall evaluate continued purpose, ownership, risk classification, model and data changes, controls, performance, incidents, obligations, value, and retirement criteria.

### 13.3 Control assessment

Applicable controls shall be assessed for design and operating effectiveness using documented procedures and sufficient evidence. Assessors shall have appropriate competence and independence from the work being assessed.

### 13.4 Internal audit

The organization shall conduct internal audits of the enterprise AI management system at planned intervals. The audit program shall consider risk, prior results, organizational change, incidents, and the importance of processes. Audit scope, criteria, methods, results, and corrective actions shall be documented.

### 13.5 Management review

Executive leadership shall review the management system at planned intervals. Inputs shall include:

- changes in context and interested-party requirements;
- progress against objectives;
- portfolio, risk, incident, exception, and compliance trends;
- capability and control performance;
- audit and assessment results;
- supplier and concentration risk;
- resources, competence, and improvement opportunities.

Outputs shall include decisions on policy, scope, objectives, resources, treatment, corrective action, and improvement.

### 13.6 Reporting

Reports shall be appropriate to the audience and distinguish facts, estimates, uncertainty, risk, and management judgment. Material risk shall be escalated promptly and not obscured by aggregate metrics.

## 14. Nonconformity and continual improvement

### 14.1 Nonconformity

When a nonconformity occurs, the organization shall:

1. control and correct it;
2. address consequences and affected parties;
3. determine cause and whether similar conditions exist elsewhere;
4. implement proportionate corrective action;
5. verify effectiveness;
6. update risk, controls, documentation, and lessons learned.

### 14.2 Continual improvement

The organization shall continually improve the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of its enterprise AI management system. Improvement inputs shall include monitoring, incidents, testing, audits, user feedback, affected-party concerns, workforce observations, threat intelligence, supplier changes, research, technology developments, and regulatory change.

### 14.3 Emergency action

Authorized personnel shall be able to restrict, suspend, isolate, roll back, or retire an AI capability when continued operation presents unacceptable risk. Emergency actions shall be logged, reviewed, and followed by controlled recovery or retirement.

## 15. Conformance

### 15.1 Conformance basis

An organization may claim conformance with ESAF-1000 only when it can demonstrate that:

- the management-system scope is defined and approved;
- all applicable `shall` requirements are implemented, or an approved exception is active;
- AI capabilities are inventoried, classified, owned, and governed through the lifecycle;
- applicable controls are selected, implemented, and assessed;
- evidence supports design and operating effectiveness;
- material nonconformities are disclosed and managed;
- the claim identifies the ESAF version, scope, date, and assessment basis.

### 15.2 Conformance statement

A conformance statement shall not imply independent certification unless an authorized, competent, and independent assessment body has performed the relevant assessment under a defined scheme.

### 15.3 Partial adoption

Organizations may describe partial adoption, alignment, or use of specific profiles, but shall not describe partial implementation as full ESAF conformance. Partial-adoption statements shall identify included and excluded requirements and the basis of evaluation.

### 15.4 Exceptions

An active exception may support conformance only when it follows Section 12.10 and the approving authority determines that residual risk remains within tolerance. An exception cannot waive applicable law or falsely represent compliance with an external scheme.

## Appendix B. Illustrative capability tiers

Organizations may use different labels, but classification criteria shall be documented and consistent.

| Tier | Description | Illustrative governance expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 0 - Experimental | Segregated research or proof of concept without material outcomes | Time-bounded approval, restricted data, named owner |
| Tier 1 - Productivity | Human-initiated assistance with limited organizational impact | Approved service, user training, data boundaries, monitoring |
| Tier 2 - Operational | Supports internal processes or recurring decisions | Formal lifecycle, validation, service ownership, periodic review |
| Tier 3 - Business Critical | Material customer, financial, workforce, security, or regulated impact | Enhanced validation, independent challenge, executive risk authority |
| Tier 4 - Mission or Safety Critical | Failure or misuse could cause severe operational, safety, rights, or systemic harm | Highest assurance, strict oversight, resilience, frequent review, tested intervention |

| Operations | Reliability, support, change, continuity, and recovery |
| Internal audit or assurance | Independent evaluation of conformance and effectiveness |

One individual may hold multiple roles where appropriate, but accountability and conflicts of interest shall remain explicit.
