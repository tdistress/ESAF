## 1. Informative status and nonclaims

ESAF-1400 is **informative**. It contains no `shall` requirements of its own
and creates no control, architecture, or assessment obligation beyond those
already established in [ESAF-1000](../framework/ESAF-1000.md),
[ESAF-1100](../controls/ESAF-1100.md), and [ESAF-1200](../architectures/ESAF-1200.md).
Where this guide describes a mandatory outcome, it is restating or pointing to
an existing `shall` requirement in one of those publications, not creating a
new one. Guidance in this document uses `should` and `may` and is intended to
help an organization sequence and operationalize existing requirements.

This guide does not:

- establish, waive, or reinterpret a normative requirement;
- approve certification, compliance, equivalence, endorsement, or production
  readiness for any capability, control, or architecture pattern;
- recommend, endorse, or imply preference for a specific commercial product,
  vendor, or service;
- substitute for the assessment methodology, evidence contracts, or maturity
  model defined in [ESAF-1500](../assessment/ESAF-1500.md);
- guarantee that following this guide is sufficient for an ESAF conformance
  claim under ESAF-1000 Section 15.

A conformance basis is established under ESAF-1000 Section 15, not by
following this guide; this guide only helps organize the underlying work.

## 2. Purpose

ESAF-1400 helps an organization move from the outcome-oriented requirements of
ESAF-1000, the assessable controls of ESAF-1100, and the reference patterns of
ESAF-1200 to a working, evidenced implementation. It sequences adoption,
connects practice to the control catalog and pattern library, describes how
implementation evidence becomes assessable, and catalogs failure modes
observed across similar enterprise programs.

| Publication | Role | Authority for |

When this guide appears to conflict with ESAF-1000, ESAF-1100, or ESAF-1200,

corrected.

## 4. How to use this guide

This guide is organized to follow a typical adoption path: sequence the work
(Section 5), connect practice to controls and patterns (Section 6), phase the
roadmap (Section 7), keep product and configuration choices vendor-neutral
(Section 8), hand evidence to ESAF-1500 (Section 9), and recognize common
failure modes early (Section 10). Organizations may adapt the order to their
own governance structures; the ESAF-1000 lifecycle and gate requirements in
its Sections 9 and 15 remain authoritative regardless of the order chosen
here.

## 5. Adoption sequence

The following sequence operationalizes the ESAF-1000 Section "How to use this
standard" outline. Each step names the ESAF-1000 area it depends on so that
implementers can trace practice back to the requirement it satisfies.

1. **Confirm management-system scope.** Align the ESAF-1000 Section 4 scope
   statement with the capabilities this implementation effort will cover.
2. **Confirm accountability.** Identify the accountable business and
   technical owners required by ESAF-1000 Section 5.5 before design work
   starts.
3. **Classify risk early.** Use the ESAF-1000 Section 6.4 classification to
   determine which ESAF-1100 control baseline and which ESAF-1200 trust-zone
   posture apply; classification should precede architecture selection, not
   follow it.
4. **Select and tailor an architecture pattern.** Use ESAF-1200's pattern
   selection method to choose a reference pattern and record deviations in an
   architecture decision record.
5. **Select applicable controls.** Map the chosen pattern's control points to
   ESAF-1100 base controls and enhancements proportionate to the risk
   classification.
6. **Implement and configure.** Build or configure the capability against the
   selected pattern and controls, keeping configuration vendor-neutral (see
   Section 8).
7. **Validate before production.** Complete the ESAF-1000 Section 9.2
   Validation stage outcomes and route findings back into design before
   requesting production authorization.
8. **Assess and evidence.** Hand implementation evidence to ESAF-1500 records
   (see Section 9) so that a determination and, where wanted, a maturity claim
   can be made.
9. **Operate and improve.** Feed monitoring, incidents, and review outcomes
   from ESAF-1000 Sections 10.9, 13, and 14 back into the next iteration of
   this sequence.

An organization may iterate steps 4 through 8 for each material change rather
than treating adoption as a single linear pass.

## 6. Mapping practice to controls and architecture patterns

Implementation work should be traceable in both directions: from a business
capability down to the controls and pattern it relies on, and from a control
or pattern back up to every capability that depends on it. A practical way to
keep that traceability current is a mapping record with, at minimum, the
following columns.

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Capability | The AI capability from the ESAF-1000 Section 8.2 inventory |
| Risk classification | The ESAF-1000 Section 6.4 classification driving scope |
| Architecture pattern | The ESAF-1200 pattern identifier and tailoring notes |
| Control points | The ESAF-1100 base controls and enhancements in scope |
| Control owner | The role accountable for each control's operation |
| Evidence source | Where ESAF-1500 evidence for the control will originate |

This mapping is implementation working material, not a control record or an
assessment result. It should be kept close to the architecture decision
record it supports and updated when the pattern, controls, or classification
change. Organizations should avoid maintaining a parallel, undocumented set of
"house" controls that duplicate or diverge from the ESAF-1100 catalog; where a
gap is found, it should be raised through the ESAF-1100 change process rather
than implemented informally.

## 7. Phased roadmap

The phases below are an illustrative sequencing aid, not a maturity model and
not a substitute for the ESAF-1500 maturity levels. An organization may use
different phase names, may run phases concurrently across independent
capabilities, and may skip a phase where existing management-system maturity
makes it unnecessary.

| Phase | Objective | Representative activities |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Foundation | Establish the management-system scaffolding this guide assumes. | Scope statement, accountable owners, inventory process, risk-classification method. |
| 1. Pilot | Prove the adoption sequence on a small, well-bounded capability set. | One or two capabilities per risk tier, one architecture pattern each, initial control mapping, first evidence records. |
| 2. Scale | Extend proven patterns and control mappings across the portfolio. | Reusable pattern and control mappings, shared platform controls, portfolio-level monitoring. |
| 3. Optimize | Improve based on assessment results, incidents, and measured outcomes. | ESAF-1500 assessments and maturity claims, control tuning, retirement of underperforming capabilities. |

A phase transition is a planning signal, not a gate; the applicable ESAF-1000
Section 9.3 lifecycle gates remain the authoritative production-readiness
control regardless of which roadmap phase a program considers itself in.

## 8. Vendor-neutral product and configuration policy

ESAF, including this guide, does not name, recommend, or imply preference for
a specific commercial product or vendor. Implementers should:

- describe requirements and mappings in terms of **capability classes** (for
  example, "model gateway," "policy-decision service," "vector store,"
  "secrets manager," "security-event platform") rather than product names;
- record actual product and version choices in implementation-local
  configuration records, model records, or an AI Bill of Materials as
  described in ESAF-1000 Section 8.3, not in ESAF standard text or in this
  guide;
- keep configuration baselines (hardening settings, network policy, logging
  scope, access-control defaults) expressed as control-satisfying outcomes so
  that a product substitution does not require rewriting the control mapping,
  only the configuration record;
- treat any inclusion of a named product in internal enablement material as
  organization-specific guidance, not as an ESAF endorsement or requirement.

This separation lets a control mapping and architecture decision survive a
vendor change, and keeps ESAF publications reusable across organizations with
different sourcing strategies.

## 9. Evidence handoff to ESAF-1500

Implementation activities routinely produce material that becomes ESAF-1500
evidence. To make the handoff predictable:

- capture evidence close to the point of implementation (configuration
  exports, test results, review sign-offs, monitoring baselines) rather than
  reconstructing it later for an assessment;
- record, at the point of capture, the fields an ESAF-1500 evidence record
  will need: source, custodian, collection method, scope, period, and known
  limitations;
- route evidence intended to support a control determination to the control
  owner identified in the Section 6 mapping, so the assessor can locate it
  without an implementation-team dependency;
- keep implementation working notes separate from the finished evidence
  record; ESAF-1500 requires every evidence record to use its schema, and
  informal notes should not be presented as if they already satisfy it;
- expect that a `draft` assessment result may reference incomplete evidence,
  but a `final` result will require the evidence sufficiency ESAF-1500
  defines; implementation teams should surface known evidence gaps early
  rather than at assessment time.

Implementation teams do not determine control satisfaction, design or
operating effectiveness, or maturity level. Those determinations belong to an
assessor applying ESAF-1500, using the evidence implementation teams help
produce.

## 10. Common failure modes

The following patterns recur across enterprise AI implementations and are
worth watching for.

- **Architecture before classification.** Selecting an ESAF-1200 pattern
  before completing risk classification, then discovering the pattern under-
  or over-controls the actual risk.
- **Control shopping.** Selecting only the ESAF-1100 controls that are already
  satisfied by existing tooling, rather than the controls proportionate to
  the classified risk.
- **Shadow adoption.** Capabilities entering production outside the ESAF-1000
  Section 8 inventory and Section 9.3 gates because pilot or experimental
  status was never formally closed out.
- **Evidence reconstruction.** Attempting to produce ESAF-1500 evidence after
  the fact for an assessment, instead of capturing it during implementation;
  reconstructed evidence is often insufficient on relevance, timeliness, or
  attribution grounds.
- **Vendor-coupled mappings.** Writing control or pattern mappings in terms of
  a specific product rather than a capability class, so a vendor change
  forces a full remapping instead of a configuration-record update.
- **Crosswalk overreach.** Treating an ESAF-1600 external-standard mapping, or
  informal alignment language in enablement material, as if it were a
  certification or compliance statement; ESAF-1000 Section 12.9 reserves that
  determination for the required independent assessment.
- **Maturity as a vanity metric.** Reporting an ESAF-1500 maturity level
  without the required prerequisite criteria and basis references, or
  averaging levels across components instead of using the lowest
  substantiated applicable level.
- **Static mapping records.** Letting the Section 6 capability-to-control
  mapping go stale after a material change, so the recorded control owner or
  evidence source no longer matches what actually runs in production.
